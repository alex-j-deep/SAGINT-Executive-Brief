## Key Article

**Economic Warfare and Military Power: How the United States Can Win the Next Era of Strategic Competition**  
Authors: Benjamin Jensen and Jacob Clayton  
Published by CSIS, May 2026

## Article Takeaways

Jacob’s article argues that economic warfare should focus on how adversaries convert resources, capital, technology, industrial capacity, and logistics into military power.

The key concept is the **conversion function**. Rather than only targeting fielded military forces, economic warfare targets the machinery behind military power. This includes flows of capital, materials, energy, technology, supply chains, finance, infrastructure, standards, and market access.

The article frames economic warfare around three core principles.

### Fungibility

The United States should identify inputs that adversaries cannot easily replace. Rare earths are a key example because China controls a dominant share of rare earth extraction and an even more important share of processing and refining capacity.

This creates a critical vulnerability for the United States and its allies.

### Elasticity

Economic pressure only works if the adversary cannot quickly reroute, substitute, or absorb the cost. The article emphasizes that enforcement must move faster than the adversary’s ability to convert restricted inputs into military capability.

The practical issue is not only whether adversaries can find substitutes, but whether the United States can detect substitution before the adversary turns those inputs into military power.

### Market Making

The United States should build deep, trusted, liquid allied markets where compliant behavior is easier, cheaper, and safer than evasion.

The goal is not simply to sanction adversaries. The goal is to make allied markets more attractive while pushing adversaries into thinner, more expensive, and more visible markets.

### Verification

Verification is the enabling condition for everything else.

Policy does not matter if the United States cannot prove where materials came from, who handled them, and whether the supply chain is compliant. In rare earths, this points to the need for transaction level visibility and chain of custody from mine to processing to end use.

Jacob believes the United States and its allies are not competing effectively at the market infrastructure level.

Key points:

- China controls and manipulates key portions of the critical minerals market.
- The U.S. focuses too much on securing supply chains and not enough on the infrastructure that sets prices, verifies provenance, enables finance, and enforces compliance.
- Traceability is not just compliance tooling. It is market shaping infrastructure.
- Trusted traceability can unlock finance, insurance, government enforcement, and allied market coordination.
- SAGINT’s platform is designed to make compliance easier and cheaper than evasion.

## SAGINT Platform

Jacob described SAGINT as a non-custodial infrastructure provider.

SAGINT does not mine, refine, validate, finance, or physically touch the assets. Instead, it creates the digital asset infrastructure and traceability system that receives validated data and makes assets compliant, bankable, insurable, and enforceable.

The platform is built around:

- UCC Chapter 12
- Controllable electronic records
- Digital titles for physical assets
- Independent validators
- Traceability from each node in non-linear supply chains
- Flat token fees
- Integration with financial and insurance products
- Government enforcement authorities

Jacob compared SAGINT to **Stripe for supply chains**.
